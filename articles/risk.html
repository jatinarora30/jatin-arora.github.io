Risk vs Uncertainty — The Most Important Distinction in Robot Safety

Most robotic systems are designed to handle risk. Very few are designed to handle uncertainty.

This distinction is subtle, but it explains why systems that perform well in simulation often fail in the real world.

Risk refers to situations where outcomes are unknown, but their probabilities are known or can be estimated.

A robot navigating around obstacles is operating under risk. Collisions are possible, but their likelihood can be modeled.

Uncertainty is fundamentally different. It refers to situations where the system does not even know what outcomes are possible, or how likely they are.

Sensor failures, unseen environments, unexpected human behavior, or model mismatch all introduce uncertainty.

The problem is that most control and learning algorithms assume the world is governed by risk, not uncertainty.

They optimize expected performance under known distributions. When those assumptions break, safety breaks with them.

Consider a mobile robot trained in simulation. It learns to avoid obstacles and reaches its goal efficiently.

In deployment, a slight delay in sensing or an unmodeled disturbance can push the system into unsafe states.

The failure is not due to poor intelligence. It is due to unhandled uncertainty.

This leads to a critical insight:

Risk can be optimized. Uncertainty must be guarded against.

A safe robotic system must therefore go beyond minimizing expected cost. It must detect when it is operating outside its assumptions, and respond conservatively.

This may involve reducing speed, increasing safety margins, switching to fallback behaviors, or stopping altogether.

As robots move into open-world environments, safety can no longer rely on accurate models alone.

It must be designed to handle what the system does not know.

The future of robot safety lies not only in better models, but in systems that remain safe even when those models are wrong.
